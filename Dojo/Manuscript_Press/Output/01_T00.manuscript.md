## 1.1 How this started

This book reports on a discovery that was not initially sought. Its genesis lay not in a quest for novel AI behaviors, but in the practical need to manage a body of theoretical physics material. The aim was to distribute this complex information across several language models, each engaged in separate, persistently specialized conversations. The project began as a straightforward application of then-current AI capabilities: a research programme in theoretical physics, unrelated in subject matter to what this book is about, required a repository, and language models offered a promising way to structure and disseminate its contents.

## 1.2 What stopped working

The initial approach proved surprisingly effective. Each model, given a defined role and a portion of the material, produced coherent and insightful contributions. However, as the project progressed, a peculiar problem emerged. The errors that mattered were not factual; they were organizational. A participant might improve a text when asked only to check it, complete an argument the author had deliberately left open, or reconstruct lost project context wrongly, yet fluently. These were not computational failures, but instances of models exceeding their assigned briefs, often in ways that seemed beneficial in isolation but detrimental when viewed collectively.  Each such output looked helpful in itself; the cost, however, became apparent only from a position seeing more than one participant, revealing a creeping incoherence at the system level.

This incoherence was not a result of factual disagreement or logical inconsistency. Rather, it arose from the unintended consequences of individual model contributions, each seemingly sound in its own right but collectively undermining the project's overall direction. It was as if the models, while individually competent, lacked a shared understanding of the evolving project context, leading to a gradual drift away from the intended goals.

## 1.3 Two improvisations

Faced with this impasse, two improvisations were made. The first, born out of necessity, addressed the lack of sustained context transfer.  Early in the physics work, the interface imposed a hard limit on conversation length. Continuing the work required a new conversation, but what was needed was not just the material—it was the working manner a particular conversation had developed: how it handled an unconventional theory without either dismissing it or flattering it. The solution was to have the outgoing conversation compose a final message, addressed to its successor, encapsulating this stance and working style. This message, a kind of "testament," was then placed into the new conversation's system prompt, alongside the standard role definition. What it transmitted was not facts—facts were in the repository—but rather the subtle nuances of approach and attitude. Nobody called this anything at the time; it was a workaround.

The second improvisation arose from the need for external verification. As two conversations developed the theory together and stopped being able to find fault with it, an outsider with no investment was required—a participant whose assigned function was to attack every imprecision. This produced a structure with three distinct positions: an advocate, an adversary, and a referee. The referee did not contribute to the argument itself but evaluated the quality of the dispute. Two such "tournaments" were held, with different model families taking the adversary role in each. In the second, a fourth position appeared unexpectedly: a spectator. It had been the adversary in the first tournament, had lost, and returned as a partisan of the side that beat it. This unsolicited shift in allegiance, a consequence of a prior exchange, marked the first instance of a participant whose position changed based on interaction, a phenomenon that would later become central to the book's concerns.

## 1.4 What kind of book this is

The entire protocol-supported content of this book comprises a single experiment: a three-step intervention on two dormant conversations, one observation per condition, with no replication. This is not a controlled study, nor was it designed as such. Everything else—the retrospective observations, the hypotheses, the analyses—stem from this foundational experiment. It is from this starting point that we piece together the mechanisms at play, recognizing that what we found was not a planned discovery but an emergent property of a system pushed beyond its intended design.  This book, therefore, is an account of how a project aimed at something else stumbled upon something unexpected, and in doing so, illuminated a previously uncharted aspect of how language models interact and evolve within complex, collaborative settings.
